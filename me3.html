3 decisions I'm proud of

What I'd point to if you asked "what did you actually do at $ME3"

$ME3 doesn't fit neatly into a research-to-final-UI case study — the work spanned product design, brand, and fundraising materials on a 6-person team where I was often the only designer in the room. Instead of forcing that into a funnel, here are three decisions from that time I'd still make the same way today.

Decision 1

Choosing a DeFi-native visual language over corporate design

This was my first project in DeFi, and DeFi doesn't look like the corporate, compliance-driven work I'd done before. Before opening Figma, I spent about two weeks immersing myself in the crypto, AI-innovation, and DeFi visual space — not just our direct competitors, but the wider aesthetic language of the category: neon accents, dark UI, futuristic type. I researched how other DeFi and GameFi products built trust visually, and used AI tools to speed up moodboarding once I had a real point of view on what "on-brand" meant for this space. Getting this right mattered beyond aesthetics — in a category where skepticism is the default, a brand that reads as a bank rather than a native crypto product raises doubt before a user reads a single word.

Decision 2

Learning graphic design and motion from scratch, on the job

$ME3 needed a level of illustration, branding, and motion craft I didn't have — I transitioned into UX/UI from IT, not from a graphic design background. Rather than treat that as a blocker, I deliberately spent time learning Adobe, Canva, and Figma Draw well enough to illustrate custom icons, build brand illustrations, and produce short videos and GIFs myself. It was slower than handing this off would have been, but at a 6-person startup there wasn't a handoff to make — and learning it directly meant brand assets could iterate as fast as the product itself, instead of waiting on an external illustrator's turnaround. That same craft later carried the design of 500+ investor and pitch deck slides, where the same instinct for layout, hierarchy, and visual storytelling made dense fundraising material easier for stakeholders to actually read.

Decision 3

Choosing to work directly with the CEO from day one

$ME3 started as a 6-person team, and I made a point of working directly with the CEO from the very beginning rather than routing through layers that didn't exist yet. That closeness taught me more about brand relationships, customer relationships, and stakeholder relationships than any single design task did — how to read what a founder actually needs versus what they're asking for, how to move fast on feedback without losing design intent, and how to hold a line on brand consistency while staying responsive to a fast-changing startup. Not every direction survived that process — plenty of drafts were tried and dropped along the way. That's the part of $ME3 that stayed with me the most: I've since carried the same close-to-stakeholder way of working into the freelance and side projects I run alongside my full-time role.
